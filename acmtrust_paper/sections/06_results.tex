Table~\ref{tab:main} shows what each rule-based judge releases. With only the platform check, Claude-built programs answer \rNoCheckCovClaude\% of the pairs, and \rNoCheckWrongClaude{} of those answers are wrong (\rNoCheckErrClaude\%). The history check leaves \rHistWrongClaude{} of them, and Vouch leaves \rGateWrongClaude, an error of \rGateErrClaude\% at \rGateCovClaude\% coverage. A random refusal at Vouch's coverage keeps the \rRandomErrClaude\% error and 3.4 points fewer correct answers, so declining the same number of answers at random would not produce Vouch's gain.

Most of these errors come from one platform. On \otf R1 programs, \rOtfHistWrong{} wrong answers pass the history check and Vouch releases none of them. Of all \rNoCheckWrongClaude{} wrong answers, \rRouteProbWrong{} follow a change of routing probabilities, which the history check cannot exercise because the recorded probabilities never vary. On \devs the history check already leaves only 5 wrong answers, and so does Vouch. The coding agent's programs, and all programs in manufacturing, give no wrong answer at all. Programs written by open-weight models mostly fail the history check: with \rNoCheckErrOllama\% error under the platform check, Vouch releases only \rGateCovOllama\% of their pairs, and a disagreement check keeps 10.6 points more correct answers.

These errors are not misreadings of the question: for every one of the \rNoCheckWrongClaude{} wrong answers, the structured change equalled the hidden correct reading. Interpretation was exact on \rInterpWf{} workflow and \rInterpMl{} manufacturing and IT questions; the seven misreadings cost only coverage (\rMlCovInterp\% instead of \rMlCovOracle\% with the correct reading). The faults lie in the programs, and in many of them: \rProgsWithWrong{} of the \rProgsClaude{} Claude-built programs give a wrong answer, Vouch removes all of them for \rProgsAllRemoved, the \rProgsWrongKept{} that keep one or two are \devs programs, and no \otf R1 program contributes more than \rOtfLargestProgWrong{} of the 243.

Vouch pays for this in correct answers. It declines \rRightLostCountClaude{} of 6{,}075 correct answers (\rRightLostClaude\%) and keeps 2.2 points fewer correct answers than the history check, 4.5 on \otf R1. The loss is largest in IT, where the history check keeps 5.4 points more with the same three wrong answers: once a program fails a test of removing a particular machine, Vouch declines every later question about that machine, and \rItAlreadyRight{} of those answers were correct (\S\ref{sec:results:families}). Generic and program-wide tests release almost exactly what the history check releases (205 and 206 wrong), so tests drawn at random from the input-use family do not find these errors; Vouch's routing and worker tests do.

A judge can lower its error by declining whatever it cannot check, so we ask why Vouch declined each wrong answer (Table~\ref{tab:families}). Of the 272 it removed, \rCaughtWrong{} were \Caught by a test on the question itself, \rAlreadyWrong{} were \Already because the program had failed the same test earlier, and \rHistWrongRemoved{} \Failhist. None was removed by abstaining. Of the \rUntestableRight{} correct answers declined as \Untestable, \rUntestableRightNoEffect{} are no-effect controls on which no test of the change could run.

An example from the routing family shows detection at work. In Run~05 of the loan process, a question asks when case c0005 would end if decision D2 sent 30\% of cases one way and 70\% the other. All six Claude-built \otf R1 loan programs pass the history check and answer 47{,}253 seconds, the recorded value; the correct answer is 207{,}014. Vouch's routing test looks at the earliest decision on that run that the new probabilities must switch: the draw printed for another case, c0004, at D2 now requires it to take the other route, but none of the programs changes c0004's records, and Vouch declines (\Caught). The programs ignore the stated probabilities, which never vary in the recorded history.

Detection also costs correct answers. Of the 430 declined, 277 come from programs that fail the history check, 43 from abstention and 110 from failed tests, which can fail on another case or input than the one the question touches. Retesting every question instead of consulting the record catches the same wrong answers and releases \rFreshRightRecovered{} more correct ones, at \rFreshExecPerQ{} instead of 1.54 program runs per question, so the record saves runs, not errors.

\subsection{Comparison with LLM judges}
\label{sec:results:llm}

Table~\ref{tab:llm} compares Vouch with LLM judges, the usual alternative to testing, each on its own subset, and Figure~\ref{fig:riskcov} shows the whole trade-off on \otf R1, where the errors are. At equal coverage the three Haiku judges accept more wrong answers than Vouch: the answer-level judge \rAnsJudgeErrClaude\% against \rGateErrAnsSub\% on the same \rAnsJudgeN{} pairs, the program-level judge \rProgJudgeErrClaude\% against 0.1\%, and the ReAct judge \rReactErrClaude\% against \rGateErrReactSub\%; all three differences are significant. No threshold closes the gap: above 5\% coverage no Haiku judge gets below 1.8\% error on \otf R1, while Vouch releases 81\% of those answers with none wrong.

Sonnet and the open-weight judges ran on 32 to 101 Claude-built pairs containing at most four wrong answers, and at equal coverage Vouch ties with all of them. On its own verdicts, Sonnet's answer-level judge trusts \rSonnetAnsOwnCov\% of these pairs with no wrong answer, against Vouch's 84.2\%; Sonnet and the Haiku ReAct judge tend to keep more correct answers than Vouch, though neither difference holds when whole runs are resampled. The gpt-oss judges keep 26 and 32 points fewer, partly because \rGptossUnparsed{} of 150 ReAct replies broke the required format. Being able to run the program did not make the ReAct judge a tester: Haiku's ran it on only \rReactRanProgHaiku\% of its pairs and Sonnet's on 11 of 50, deciding the rest from reading alone.

\subsection{When the rules change}
\label{sec:results:reuse}

Programs are meant to be reused after the rules change. Under P, where one pool has one more worker, the programs answer \rPolPNoCheckCov\% of the questions under the platform check alone, and \rPolPNoCheckErr\% of those answers are wrong. Every program fails the history check on every P run, so Vouch releases nothing, while a disagreement check keeps \rPolPDisCov\% of the questions without a wrong answer. Under H, where the rules are reworded but mean the same, Vouch treats the text as a new policy, tests again (1.45 extra runs per question), and releases \rPolHGateCov\% of the answers with none wrong; the history check alone releases \rPolHHistCov\% with 35 wrong. No P or H document shares a fingerprint with a test document, so nothing recorded under the original rules was carried over, and this set does not test whether recorded results transfer correctly across policies.

\subsection{Cost}
\label{sec:results:cost}

Vouch makes no model calls beyond the interpretation every judge shares; its cost is program runs. It used 1.54 extra runs per question on average (median \rGateExecMedian), fewer than generic tests (2.62) or program-wide tests (3.21) because results are reused within a run (\rFreshExecPerQ{} without the record), and all rule-based judges together took a median of \rGateSecPerQ{} seconds per question. The Haiku answer-level judge cost \$\rAnsJudgeUSDPerRow{} and \rAnsJudgeSecPerRow{} seconds per pair, the Haiku ReAct judge \$\rReactUSDPerRow{} and \rReactSecPerRow{} seconds, and Sonnet's judges \$0.052 and \$0.064. The whole study, including development and failed runs, cost about \$\rTotalUSD{} in model calls.
